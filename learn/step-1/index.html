What Is a Blockchain?

7 min readUpdated 2026-10-01By the 7Blockchain editorial team

7 key takeaways

  1. A blockchain is a shared ledger copied across thousands of computers (nodes).
  2. Transactions are grouped into blocks; each block carries the fingerprint (hash) of the previous one.
  3. Changing an old block breaks every hash after it — that's what makes history tamper-evident.
  4. Consensus rules (Proof of Work, Proof of Stake) decide who adds the next block.
  5. Public blockchains are permissionless: anyone can read, transact or run a node.
  6. The ledger is transparent; identities are pseudonymous addresses.
  7. Blockchains trade speed for trust-minimisation — that's a feature, not a bug.

The one-sentence version

A blockchain is a database that many strangers maintain together, where every entry is linked to the one before it so the past cannot be silently edited. Banks keep one private ledger; a blockchain keeps thousands of identical public copies and uses math, not managers, to keep them in sync.

Blocks, hashes and the chain

Every few seconds or minutes, pending transactions are bundled into a block. The block is run through a hash function that produces a unique fingerprint. The next block stores that fingerprint. Change one character in an old transaction and its hash changes, which changes the next block's hash, and so on — the whole chain after the edit stops matching the copies everyone else holds.

Who adds the next block?

Nodes must agree on one version of history. Proof of Work (Bitcoin) makes miners spend electricity solving a puzzle; cheating costs more than it pays. Proof of Stake (Ethereum, Solana, Cardano) makes validators lock up coins as a bond that is destroyed if they misbehave. Both replace a trusted administrator with an economic penalty.

Why it matters

Because no single party controls the ledger, a blockchain can host money (Bitcoin), programmable contracts (Ethereum), and ownership records (NFTs, tokenised assets) that work the same for everyone, everywhere, 24/7. The cost is throughput: Bitcoin processes ~7 transactions per second on its base layer, Visa thousands. Layer-2 networks and newer Layer-1s narrow the gap — see our Top 7 Layer-1 and Layer-2 rankings.

What a blockchain is not

It is not anonymous (most are pseudonymous and fully traceable), not free (every transaction pays a fee), and not automatically trustworthy (the code is only as good as its authors and audits). Keep these three in mind and most scams become obvious.

Frequently asked questions

Is blockchain the same as Bitcoin?

No. Bitcoin is the first and largest application of a blockchain. Thousands of other blockchains exist with different rules and purposes.

Can a blockchain be hacked?

The ledger itself is practically immutable on large networks. What gets hacked are exchanges, wallets, bridges and smart contracts built on top — which is why Step 4 and Step 7 are about security.

Do I need to run a node?

No. Wallets talk to nodes for you. Running one is optional and improves your privacy and the network's resilience.
